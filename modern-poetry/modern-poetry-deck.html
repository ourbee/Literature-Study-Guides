Modern Poetry

Rebellion, fragmentation, and the poets who rebuilt the form for a broken century.

English Literature · DSCC1
Key poets: Eliot · Pound · Yeats · Auden · the 1930s poets, and others
THE ARGUMENT

Modern poetry breaks with inherited form because the old forms could no longer hold a fractured, war-shaken world.

Modern Poetry · DSCC12
CONTEXT

What makes poetry "modern"?

Modern Poetry emerges in the early twentieth century, against industrialisation, the First World War, and the collapse of settled social and religious certainties. Poets stopped assuming that traditional metre and rhyme could carry that experience honestly.

FOUR RECURRING FEATURES
  • Free verse and structural experiment, in place of fixed metre and rhyme.
  • Fragmented, disjointed syntax — the poem's form mirrors a broken world.
  • Themes of alienation, disillusionment, and existential doubt.
  • Denser, more layered imagery and symbolism, asking more of the reader.

THE MAJOR POETS · T.S. ELIOT (I)

Eliot and The Waste Land

Eliot's The Waste Land (1922) is the landmark Modernist poem. Its opening line famously calls April "the cruellest month" — a poem that walks through the desolation of a post-war world rather than describing it from outside.

THE METHOD
  • Stream-of-consciousness narration, moving without warning between voices.
  • A collage of literary, mythic, and cultural allusion — the poem assumes a whole library behind it.
  • Multiple, overlapping speakers instead of a single stable "I".

Analogy: reading The Waste Land is like tuning an old radio — voices, languages, and songs cut in and out, and the "static" between them is part of the meaning.

THE MAJOR POETS · T.S. ELIOT (II)

The critic behind the poet

Eliot was as influential as a critic as he was as a poet. He argued for the "objective correlative" — the idea that a poem should render emotion through a precise set of objects, images, or events, rather than stating the feeling directly.

He also argued for an "impersonal" poetry, where the poet's private self is not the subject of the poem. His later sequence Four Quartets turns from fragmentation towards meditations on time, memory, and spiritual redemption.

TEACHING PROMPT

Ask students: if a poet cannot simply write "I feel despair," what image might do that work instead? Try it with a feeling of their own choosing.

THE MAJOR POETS · EZRA POUND

Pound and Imagism

Pound led the Imagist movement, which prized a single, precise picture over explanation or ornament. His two-line poem "In a Station of the Metro" sets faces in a crowd against petals on a wet, black bough — nothing is explained, the image is left to do all the work.

TWO ROLES, ONE CAREER
  • Poet: strips language back to clear, sharp visual moments.
  • Editor: shaped the careers of Eliot and H.D., among others.
  • His long sequence The Cantos ranges across history, economics, and language — and his politics remain deeply controversial.

Analogy: an Imagist poem works like a photograph, not a paragraph — it shows a single frozen instant and trusts the viewer to feel the rest.

THE MAJOR POETS · W.B. YEATS (I)

From myth to modernity

Yeats's career spans two very different poets. His early work draws on Irish myth and Romantic symbolism — fairies, legends, a dreamlike Ireland. As the century turns violent, his voice hardens into something more public, political, and unsettled.

WHY THE SHIFT MATTERS
  • He lived through the Easter Rising, the Irish War of Independence, and the First World War — history forced its way into his poems.
  • He co-founded the Abbey Theatre and was a central figure in the Irish Literary Revival.
Modern Poetry · DSCC17
THE MAJOR POETS · W.B. YEATS (II)

"The Second Coming"

"The Second Coming" (1919) is Yeats's most quoted vision of a civilisation coming apart: "Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold." Written just after the First World War and amid revolutionary violence in Ireland, it imagines history turning like a widening spiral (a "gyre") that has spun out of control.

The poem fuses personal anxiety with public history — a hallmark of Yeats's mature style, and one reason he is read alongside Eliot as a founder of Modernist poetry rather than simply a late Romantic.

TEACHING PROMPT

Ask students where they see "things falling apart" in their own moment, and whether Yeats's image of the widening gyre still feels usable today.

THE MAJOR POETS · W.H. AUDEN (I)

The personal and the public

Auden's gift was fusing the intimate with the historical. His elegy "In Memory of W.B. Yeats", written on Yeats's death in 1939, mourns a poet while asking what poetry itself can do in a darkening political moment — its famous claim that "poetry makes nothing happen" is less despair than a precise definition of poetry's real power.

A GENERATION'S CONSCIENCE
  • His poetry repeatedly returns to moral and political crisis, set against the 1930s slide towards war.
  • He wrote with unusual clarity about anxiety, love, and public duty in the same poem.
Modern Poetry · DSCC19
THE MAJOR POETS · W.H. AUDEN (II)

A style that keeps changing

Auden's early poems can be symbolic and deliberately obscure. Over his career the style opens out — plainer, more conversational, closer to speech. His later poem "The Shield of Achilles" retells Homer's shield of Achilles as a vision of a bleak, mechanised modern war, asking what a civilisation actually chooses to honour.

Analogy: reading early to late Auden is like watching a lecturer relax across a term — the ideas get no simpler, but the sentences do.

THE MAJOR POETS · THE 1930s POETS

The "Auden Group"

Auden did not write alone. A loose circle of younger, left-leaning poets — often called the "Auden Group" or, mockingly, "MacSpaunday" (MacNeice–Spender–Auden–Day-Lewis) — dominated British poetry through the 1930s, the decade of the Depression, fascism's rise, and the Spanish Civil War.

WHO'S WHO
  • Louis MacNeice — wary of political certainty, alert to ordinary detail; "Bagpipe Music" and "Autumn Journal".
  • Stephen Spender — openly political, drawn to industrial imagery; "The Pylons" gave the group its nickname, "the Pylon poets".
  • C. Day-Lewis — Marxist-leaning verse calling poetry into public service.

Shared ground: plain, direct language; industrial and technological imagery (pylons, engines, aeroplanes); and a poetry openly engaged with contemporary politics, unlike Eliot's more allusive detachment.

OTHER VOICES OF MODERNISM

Two more directions

H.D. (HILDA DOOLITTLE)

A core Imagist. "Oread" builds a whole scene from a handful of exact words. Her poetry returns to classical myth, nature, and — later, in Trilogy and Helen in Egypt — a more narrative, mythological voice, alongside an engagement with psychoanalysis and questions of gender.

LANGSTON HUGHES

A leading voice of the Harlem Renaissance. "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" ties African-American identity to a long, deep history. His verse borrows the rhythms of blues and jazz and uses vernacular speech to write black American life directly, in its own voice.

OTHER VOICES OF MODERNISM

Sound, form, and precision

DYLAN THOMAS

Welsh, musical, emotionally intense. "Do Not Go Gentle into That Good Night" faces death with rhythmic, defiant language rooted in his Welsh landscape and voice.

E.E. CUMMINGS & ELIZABETH BISHOP

cummings breaks grammar and typography itself — unconventional layout carrying tender, playful feeling. Bishop, by contrast, is controlled and observational; "One Art" turns loss into quiet, exact understatement.

WHERE WE HAVE GOT TO

Modern Poetry did not just change what poems said — it changed what a poem was allowed to look like, and every later poet writes in its aftermath.
